Around the same time, I had a business trip lined up to Paraguay. The main objective was work — meetings, the usual. But Paraguay has this gravitational pull: duty-free shops with imported gear at prices that are simply not fair. I walked out with a Victorinox I had been eyeing for months. Then Pontal do Sul and Ilha do Mel. A few days hiking the trails on the island — no roads, no cars, ferries only. Your gear actually matters there: the knife you grab for camp prep, the flashlight for trail navigation at dusk, the layers you packed. Both trips did the same thing: moved EDC from hobby to habit.

## +2400 km in a Week

Paraguay and back, then straight to Paraná. I put over 2400 km on the Civic in a single week.

The Civic is a naturally aspirated 2.0L — an engine that makes power the hard way. NA engines can't just brute-force more air in. The cylinder is limited by atmospheric pressure, so engineers squeeze power out by eliminating every restriction: intake geometry, exhaust tuning, valve timing, combustion chamber shape. The gains come from harmonics — carefully timed pressure waves in the intake and exhaust that help pack the cylinder at specific RPMs. Every horsepower earned through precision.

Turbocharged engines — like the 1.4 TSI in a Golf or A3 — take the opposite philosophy. A compressor shoves air in under massive positive pressure. The cylinder fills easily, almost effortlessly. As turbo enthusiasts say: *"Air is free."*

Turbos are easier to make power with — of course they're quicker on paper. But at what cost?

Turbo torque is unpredictable. There's lag — you ask, it hesitates, then it shoves. Worse, the torque arrives at the wrong moment: crack the throttle mid-corner and you get a spike of torque exactly when the front wheels are already loaded, exactly when you need modulation, not violence. Understeer on corner exits. The car pushing wide when you want to rotate.

NA just sings. Linear from idle to the 8000 RPM redline. You always know what you're getting. The power follows the throttle like a conversation — ask a little, get a little. The engine rewards finesse — a driver who is in touch with their car.

Turbo is brute force. NA is craft.

## More vs Better

The car world has an escape hatch: if you want more power, bolt a turbo on. Swap the injectors, tune the ECU, make more. Problem solved by addition.

The cyclist has no such escape hatch. You can't strap a compressor to your nose. Power is grown — slowly, over years of training, recovery, and adaptation. The body is the engine, and it doesn't take bolt-ons.

So cycling teaches a different obsession: efficiency. Every watt you produce has to go somewhere useful. Better position on the bike reduces drag. Lighter wheels reduce rotational inertia. Smoother pedal stroke wastes less energy at the top and bottom of the rotation. The gains are marginal and cumulative — and every one of them is about doing *better*, not doing *more*.

Where the turbo engineer asks "how do I add power?", the cyclist asks "where am I losing it?" Less is more. Efficiency is the only lever. And the deepest form of efficiency isn't doing things faster — it's doing less of what is of no use.

## Muda

I learned this the hard way at Toyota — TCI, Cambridge, Ontario. The Toyota Production System doesn't start with speed. It starts with **muda**: waste. Any activity that consumes resources without creating value. The seven types are almost boring in how obvious they are once you see them — overproduction, waiting, unnecessary motion, defects, excess inventory. The insight isn't the list. It's the discipline to actually hunt them down, one by one, and kill them.

TPS doesn't ask "how do we go faster?" It asks "where are we wasting?" The turbo answer to manufacturing would be: bigger machines, more shifts, more inventory buffer. Toyota's answer: eliminate the motion that doesn't build the car. The two mindsets produce very different factories.

The cyclist, the NA engine, the Toyota production line — all optimizing the same way. Not more input. Less waste.

2400 km gives you time to think about this.
