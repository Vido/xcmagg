Frequently Asked Questions

Each row is a cassette cog (smallest = hardest at top, largest = easiest at bottom). Each column is a chainring. Every cell shows the selected metric — speed, ratio, gear inches, or development — for that combination at your chosen cadence.

Color: green = easy gear (low ratio), red = hard gear (high ratio). The gradient is normalized across all cells so relative differences are immediately visible.

Dashed border: marks gears whose ratio is within ±2% of another combination in a different chainring — these are near-duplicate gears you'd cross-chain to reach.

Development (m/rev) is the distance the bike travels per full pedal revolution. It equals the gear ratio × wheel circumference. A development of 5.0 m/rev means one complete crank revolution moves the bike forward 5 metres. This is the most practical single number for comparing gearing across different wheel sizes — unlike gear inches, development is in SI units and directly tells you ground distance per stroke.

Gear inches is the traditional Anglo-Saxon equivalent of development: the diameter (in inches) of a hypothetical direct-drive wheel that would give the same mechanical advantage. Gear inches = (chainring ÷ cog) × wheel diameter in inches. A gear of 60 gear inches means the drivetrain is equivalent to a 60-inch-diameter penny-farthing wheel. Still widely used in road and track cycling contexts.

Overlap means two different chainring/cog combinations produce nearly the same gear ratio. For example, a 50×17 and a 34×11.5 both yield roughly the same development. In practice, overlapping gears waste cassette range — you shift between chainrings only to find the same gear you already had. The dashed border in the matrix highlights these duplicates so you can see how much unique range your drivetrain actually offers. Well-designed 2x drivetrains have some overlap in the middle of the cassette (for smooth front shifts) but minimal overlap at the extremes.

Use the Speed display at your minimum sustainable cadence (e.g. 60 rpm) and check the bottom row (largest cog). If the speed is lower than you'd ever ride, your bailout gear is adequate. If you can barely spin the lowest gear on a 15% grade at 60 rpm, you need a larger cog or smaller chainring.

A practical rule: for every 1% of gradient increase, you need roughly 6% more gear reduction to maintain the same cadence and power. Going from a 10% climb to a 20% climb roughly doubles the required gear ratio reduction.

1x advantages: simpler shifting (rear only), lighter front end, no front derailleur to adjust, wider chain line compatibility, less cross-chaining. Modern wide-range cassettes (10-52 or 10-50) give adequate range for most MTB use.

2x advantages: tighter gear steps in the middle of the range (better for sustained road power where small cadence jumps matter), less total cog range needed (smaller individual jumps between cogs), and more torque efficiency on mid-range cogs vs very large cogs. Road cyclists typically prefer 2x because they care about fine-grained cadence control more than extreme range.

Use this matrix to compare: load a 1x setup vs a 2x setup and look at Development — count the unique steps and check for gaps that would force a large cadence change between gears.
